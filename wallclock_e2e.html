End-to-end experiments — wall-clock controlled

Every arm gets the same 30 minutes of wall clock on a held-out MLE-bench program, not the same number of turns. Turn control charges the interpreter nothing for the minutes it spends executing; wall clock prices it, which is the only setting where an execution-free channel can show an advantage. Library = 430 performance + 245 error + 56 runtime rubrics (731). Coder claude-haiku-4-5, world model claude-opus-5, selector = the coder's own model. 97 arm-runs, 20 programs, 12 competitions, frozen 5/15 split.

Headline: the interpreter wins. It improves 58% of programs against 25% for the best world-model arm. The mechanism the experiment was built to show is real — the same budget buys ~8x more turns — but it does not convert into score, and the figures below isolate why.

Left: unconditional performance, where a broken program counts against the arm. Middle: full outcome breakdown. Right: the selection trap — every world-model arm looks within ~2-3% of the interpreter on the programs it finished, and 37-47 percentage points worse once the programs it destroyed are counted. The right panel is the honest one.

Efficiency: the gain is real and it does not become signal

The interpreter takes 10 turns; the world-model arms take 76-96 in the same 1800s. But turns are not signals: ~87% of world-model turns find no violation, so 79 turns deliver 6 actionable fires against the interpreter's 10 executions. The speed advantage is genuine and it never reaches the coder. Only the interpreter saturates its budget (97%), and it does so by waiting — 1528 of 1751s inside subprocess.

What the rubric library actually did

Retrieval reaches all three families after the stratified-slot fix (error rubrics went from 0.1% to 47% of slots). The newest and smallest family, runtime_cost, fires most per retrieval. The right panel is the uncomfortable one: 83% of the library never fires once, which makes 'mine more rubrics' the weakest available lever.

Precision is the binding constraint

Left: signals x causal precision. The interpreter delivers ~10 signals at ~1.0 precision; the world model 6 at 0.196 — an ~8x effective-signal deficit that is the whole result. The hatched bar is an ESTIMATE of a validated ~150-rubric library, not a measurement. Middle/right: causal gating cuts breakage 10 -> 7 of 19 and wins head-to-head on 4 of 7 programs both libraries scored, from 5% of the rubrics — but 22 rubrics leave 83% of the budget unspent. Precision and coverage are multiplicative and the project has each one separately.

Race diagnostics — and the confound check

The race arm is 96.2% world model: it wins 1616 of 1680 races, and the interpreter's 64 wins are all fast crashes (4.4s median). So the race arm's results are NOT confounded by interpreter help — it is the world-model channel, measured. The right panel shows the ceiling every world-model arm hits: ~87% of turns say nothing.

Corrections to earlier readings of this experiment

  1. Runtime rubrics select hard programs; they do not break them. corrected An earlier pass reported that 11 of 11 programs where a runtime rubric fired ended broken, and attributed it to the feedback. The interpreter — which sees no rubrics — also fails on those programs (scored 3/7 where a runtime rubric fired, 12/12 where none did). That is selection, not causation. The pre-registered falsifier ("if it still breaks every program it touches, ablate the family") would have deleted the most predictive family in the library; a one-arm falsifier cannot separate selection from causation.
  2. The race arm uses 66% of its budget, not 18%. corrected The 18% figure came from 7 rows. Only the 22-rubric gated library genuinely exhausts early (17%). Coverage is decisive at the small end and mild at the full-library end.
  3. Gating the 430-rubric library is blocked, and not by compute. The rubric-to-source-program link did not survive a machine migration (all 430 exported rows have empty task_id and no proposed_patch), and the DL competitions have no measured noise floor, so gate_causal.py's ship rule cannot be evaluated on them.

What would close the gap

Effective signal is fires x precision, and the interpreter sits at ~10. Scaling rubrics alone cannot get there: at precision 0.196 it would take ~50 fires per program. The unblocked levers are (1) a train-side fire-to-outcome filter — label each fire by whether the arm underperformed the interpreter on that same program, derived on train competitions and applied to eval, which needs no provenance recovery; (2) a k-vote world-model judge, firing only on majority agreement, which the budget already affords at 62% utilisation. Both are faithful to the method rather than tuned against the eval set. A filter fitted on eval and then reported on eval would be leakage, so the train-side version is the only honest form.
